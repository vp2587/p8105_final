Full Report

1) Introduction / Motivation

Breast cancer is a major global health burden and a leading cause of cancer-related death among women. While clinical characteristics such as tumour stage and histology inform prognosis, molecular biomarkers may offer additional insight into disease progression and survival. In this project, we describe patterns of clinical features and protein biomarkers in the BRCA dataset, assess whether biomarker levels differ across patient groups, build a regression model to predict survival status, and conduct basic survival analysis to explore factors associated with follow-up outcomes.

2) Data

Source and study population.

This project uses a de-identified, publicly available breast cancer dataset obtained from Kaggle (https://www.kaggle.com/datasets/amandam1/breastcancerdataset/code). The dataset includes 334 patients with primary breast cancer who underwent surgical treatment. For each patient, the file contains demographic information (age, gender), tumour characteristics (tumour stage, histology, ER/PR/HER2 receptor status), several protein biomarkers (Protein1–Protein4), treatment information (type of surgery), and follow-up data (date of surgery, date of last visit, and vital status).

Before analysis, observations with missing patient_id were removed, as these corresponded to empty rows at the bottom of the file. For the survival analysis, we restricted to patients with non-missing dates of surgery and last follow-up, non-missing vital status, and non-negative follow-up time, resulting in 317 patients included in the Kaplan–Meier and log-rank analyses.

Data import and cleaning

brca_df_raw = read_csv("BRCA.csv") |> 
  janitor::clean_names() |> 
  filter(!is.na(patient_id)) #remove empty rows at the bottom

Variables

After clean_names(), your key columns are:

  • patient_id: Unique identifier for each patient.
  • age: Age at time of surgery (years), treated as a continuous covariate.
  • gender: Patient sex (factor).
  • protein1–protein4: Continuous protein biomarker measurements.
  • tumour_stage: Clinical tumour stage at diagnosis (I, II, III), treated as an ordered factor.
  • histology: Histological subtype of the breast tumour (categorical).
  • er_status: Oestrogen receptor (ER) status (e.g., Positive/Negative).
  • pr_status: Progesterone receptor (PR) status.
  • her2_status: HER2 receptor status.
  • surgery_type: Type of surgery performed (e.g., lumpectomy, mastectomy).

Time variables

  • Clarify outcome: patient status (Alive/Dead).
  • date_of_surgery: Calendar date of the primary breast cancer surgery.
  • date_of_last_visit: Date of last recorded follow-up visit.
  • patient_status: Vital status at last follow-up (Alive or Dead).

The primary outcome for this project is patient_status, indicating whether the patient was alive or dead at last follow-up. For survival analysis, we use date_of_surgery and date_of_last_visit to define follow-up time (in days) and combine this with patient_status to create a time-to-event and censoring indicator.

Clean data types

brca = brca_df_raw |>

    tumour_stage = factor(tumour_stage,

    er_status    = factor(er_status),
    pr_status    = factor(pr_status),
    her2_status  = factor(her2_status),
    surgery_type = factor(surgery_type),
    patient_status = factor(patient_status,
                            levels = c("Alive", "Dead")),
    #date variables
    date_of_surgery     = as.Date(date_of_surgery, format = "%d-%b-%y"),
    date_of_last_visit  = as.Date(date_of_last_visit, format = "%d-%b-%y"),

    # survival
    follow_up_time = as.numeric(date_of_last_visit - date_of_surgery),
    event = case_when(
      patient_status == "Dead"  ~ 1L,
      patient_status == "Alive" ~ 0L,
      TRUE                      ~ NA_integer_
    )
  )

This pie chart summarizes the distribution of histologic tumour types in the dataset. The largest slice corresponds to infiltrating ductal carcinoma, reflecting that most patients in this cohort have IDC, while infiltrating lobular carcinoma and mucinous carcinoma make up smaller proportions. This mirrors the expected pattern in clinical practice, where IDC is the most common invasive breast cancer subtype.

Infiltrating ductal carcinoma (IDC) is the most common breast cancer type. It begins in the milk ducts and grows into the surrounding breast tissue. As it progresses, IDC can spread through the lymphatic system or bloodstream to other parts of the body.

Mucinous carcinoma is a breast cancer subtype in which tumour cells grow within mucin, the main component of mucus. Mucin normally supports healthy cell function, but in this tumour type, the mucin produced by cancer cells becomes part of the mass. Although mucinous tumours can occur elsewhere in the body, they most often appear in the breast.

Infiltrating lobular carcinoma (ILC) starts in the lobules, the glands that produce milk. Because it is an invasive cancer, it extends beyond the lobule into nearby tissue, and over time can spread to other parts of the body.

4) Multiple linear regression: Predicting patient status

Model specification

  • Outcome: patient_status (Alive vs Dead).
  • Predictors: age, tumour_stage, surgery_type, selected proteins.
  • Brief rationale for selected predictors (based on EDA).

Model fitting

brca =
  brca %>% 
  mutate(
    target = ifelse(patient_status == "Dead", 1, 0)
  )

brca_mlr =
  brca %>%  
  glm(target ~ age + tumour_stage + surgery_type + protein1 + protein2 +
        protein3 + protein4, data = .,
      family = binomial(link = "logit")
  )

We fit a multiple logistic regression to predict mortality using clinical predictors such as age, tumour_stage, surgery_type, and the protein biomarkers (protein1-protein4). These predictors were selected based on exploratory analyses showing differences in survival outcomes across different groups.

Cross-validation

set.seed(123)

cv_df = 
  crossv_mc(brca, 5) %>% 
  mutate(
    train = map(train, as_tibble),
    test  = map(test,  as_tibble)
  ) %>% 
  mutate(
    mlr_mod = map(
      .x = train,
      ~ glm(
          target ~ age + tumour_stage + surgery_type +
            protein1 + protein2 + protein3 + protein4,
          data   = .x,
          family = binomial(link = "logit")
        )
    ),
    pred_prob = map2(
      .x = mlr_mod,
      .y = test,
      ~ predict(.x, newdata = .y, type = "response")
    ),
    auc = map2_dbl(
      .x = pred_prob,
      .y = test,
      ~ pROC::auc(pROC::roc(.y$target, .x))
    )
  )

cv_summary =
  cv_df %>% 
  summarize(
    mean_auc = mean(auc),
    sd_auc   = sd(auc)
  )

To assess generalizability, we performed a 5-fold cross-validation. The average cross-validated AUC was 0.543 with a standard deviation of 0.058. These values suggest that this model was minimally better than randomly predicting patient status.

Results

Overall survival

The overall Kaplan–Meier curve shows a gradual decline in survival probability over the follow-up period. Most patients remain alive during the first part of follow-up, and the curve flattens out after roughly 4 years, indicating that a sizeable fraction of patients survive long-term. The median survival time is approximately 1500 days, meaning that half of the cohort is expected to be alive beyond this time point.The step-wise drops in the curve correspond to observed deaths, and the tick marks indicate censored observations (patients last seen alive at that time).

Survival by tumour stage

Kaplan–Meier curves stratified by tumour stage (I, II, III) suggest that patients with more advanced disease tend to have poorer survival (Figure Y). Stage I patients generally have the highest estimated survival probabilities throughout follow-up, Stage II patients are intermediate, and Stage III patients have the lowest survival, especially after about 1,000 days.

A log-rank test comparing the three stages gives a p-value of 0.10. This is larger than the conventional 0.05 threshold, so there is no strong statistical evidence of a difference in survival across tumour stages in this dataset. However, the pattern of the curves is consistent with the expected clinical trend of worse survival for higher stage disease.

Survival by HER2 status

Kaplan–Meier curves stratified by HER2 status (positive vs negative) are almost completely overlapping (Figure Z). The log-rank test yields a p-value of 0.97, indicating no evidence of a difference in survival between HER2-positive and HER2-negative patients in this sample. This result should be interpreted cautiously because the HER2-positive group is relatively small (29 patients vs 288 HER2-negative), so the comparison may have limited power to detect modest differences.

7) Discussion

Across this cohort of 334 surgically treated breast cancer patients, we found that basic clinical characteristics and the four available protein biomarkers carried only modest prognostic information for survival status at last follow-up. Descriptively, most patients were middle-aged or older, had infiltrating ductal carcinoma, and presented with stage I–II disease. Age did not differ meaningfully between patients who were alive versus dead at last contact, and contingency‐table analyses showed no statistically significant association between survival status and either tumour stage or histologic subtype, despite patterns that were directionally consistent with worse outcomes in higher stages.

The protein biomarkers also showed limited differentiation across clinical groups. Mean expression levels of Protein1–Protein4 were similar by stage and by histology, and one-way ANOVA models (with assumptions reasonably satisfied) did not detect significant group differences after multiple-testing correction. Correlation analyses suggested that the four proteins were not perfectly redundant but also did not reveal any single marker with clearly distinct behaviour. In multivariable logistic regression, a model including age, tumour stage, surgery type, and all four proteins achieved only modest discrimination for death versus survival on cross-validation, indicating that this combination of clinical and biomarker information explains only part of the variability in outcomes.

Time-to-event analyses told a similar story. The overall Kaplan–Meier curve showed relatively good long-term survival, with a gradual decline in survival probability over several years. Stratified curves by tumour stage displayed the expected ordering (stage III tending to have poorer survival than stages I and II), but the log-rank test did not provide strong statistical evidence for differences between stages in this dataset. Survival curves by HER2 status were almost completely overlapping, and the log-rank test again showed no evidence of separation. Taken together, these results suggest that, in this relatively small observational dataset, conventional clinical variables and this limited protein panel provide only weak additional prognostic information, underscoring the need for larger cohorts and richer molecular profiling to more precisely characterise factors that drive breast cancer outcomes.
