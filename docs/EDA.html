Carcinoma Type Classification

carcinoma_type_pie =
  brca %>% 
  count(histology) %>% 
  mutate(
    prop  = n / sum(n),
    label = scales::percent(prop, accuracy = 0.1)
  ) %>% 
  ggplot(aes(x = "", y = prop, fill = histology)) +
  geom_col(color = "white") +
  coord_polar(theta = "y") +
  geom_text(aes(label = label),
            position = position_stack(vjust = 0.5),
            size = 3) +
  labs(title = "Carcinoma / Histology Classification",
       fill  = "Histology") +
  theme_void() +
  theme(plot.title = element_text(hjust = 0.5))

carcinoma_type_pie

This pie chart summarizes the distribution of histologic tumour types in the dataset. The largest slice corresponds to infiltrating ductal carcinoma, reflecting that most patients in this cohort have IDC, while infiltrating lobular carcinoma and mucinous carcinoma make up smaller proportions. This mirrors the expected pattern in clinical practice, where IDC is the most common invasive breast cancer subtype.

Infiltrating ductal carcinoma (IDC) is the most common breast cancer type. It begins in the milk ducts and grows into the surrounding breast tissue. As it progresses, IDC can spread through the lymphatic system or bloodstream to other parts of the body.

Mucinous carcinoma is a breast cancer subtype in which tumour cells grow within mucin, the main component of mucus. Mucin normally supports healthy cell function, but in this tumour type, the mucin produced by cancer cells becomes part of the mass. Although mucinous tumours can occur elsewhere in the body, they most often appear in the breast.

Infiltrating lobular carcinoma (ILC) starts in the lobules, the glands that produce milk. Because it is an invasive cancer, it extends beyond the lobule into nearby tissue, and over time can spread to other parts of the body.

We first compared age between patients who were alive and those who had died at last follow up. Mean age was 58.8 years among survivors and 59.2 years among those who died, with very similar standard deviations. A Welch two-sample t-test (t = −0.19, p = 0.85) showed no evidence of a difference in mean age between the two groups. Based on this, age does not appear to be a strong discriminator of survival status in this cohort, although we still consider it clinically important enough to include as a covariate in later models.

We next examined whether survival status was associated with tumour stage using a chi-square test of independence. Counts of alive and dead patients were similar across stages I, II, and III. The chi-square test (χ² = 1.09, df = 4, p = 0.90) provided no statistical evidence that the distribution of survival status differs by stage in this dataset. This suggests that, although more advanced disease is clinically expected to have worse outcomes, our sample does not show a strong stage–mortality association, possibly due to limited sample size or follow-up time. We still retain tumour stage as a key predictor in our logistic regression because of its clear clinical relevance.

To assess how similar the four protein biomarkers are to one another, we computed a correlation matrix for Protein1–Protein4. This helps us evaluate whether the proteins provide overlapping information or capture different biological signals. Overall, the correlations were modest to moderate in magnitude. Importantly, no protein pair showed a very strong (|r| > 0.8) correlation, indicating that none of the biomarkers are redundant. This supports the inclusion of multiple protein markers in downstream models while keeping collinearity concerns relatively low.

The patterns and correlations uncovered above — including moderate relationships among certain proteins and no strong differences in biomarker levels by tumor stage/histology — guide our next steps. With this evidence, we proceed to fit a logistic regression to predict patient status, followed by ANOVA to test for differences in biomarker expression across clinical groups, and finally survival analysis.
